Solar Loan Calculator

This solar loan calculator compares your monthly loan payment with estimated bill savings. A $20,000 loan at 6.99% APR over 20 years costs $154.94 a month and $17,185.54 in total interest. An 8 kW Texas system saves about $160.78 a month at EIA’s June 2026 price of 15.94¢, leaving you $5.84 ahead in year one.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Rooftop Payback

How much is a monthly solar loan payment?

The payment follows the standard amortization formula: loan amount × monthly rate ÷ (1 − (1 + monthly rate)^−months). Enter the APR from your loan offer rather than an advertised rate.

Term changes the payment and the total interest in opposite directions. The same $20,000 at 6.99% costs:

TermMonthly paymentTotal interest
10 years$232.11$7,853.67
20 years$154.94$17,185.54

The FTC suggests asking any lender what you will pay up front, what APR you will pay, and how the payments are calculated (FTC). With the federal residential credit gone for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025 (IRS), check whether an offer still assumes one.

Will solar savings cover the loan payment?

Whether savings cover the payment depends mostly on your electricity price. The calculator estimates monthly savings as system size × yearly kWh per kW × price ÷ 12, using EIA’s June 2026 state price and PVGIS output at the state capital.

With the same $20,000, 20-year, 6.99% loan and an 8 kW system:

  • Texas: savings of $160.78 against a $154.94 payment, about $5.84 a month ahead.
  • California: savings of $382.14 at 34.74¢, about $227.20 a month ahead.

Shorten the Texas loan to 10 years and the payment of $232.11 exceeds savings by $71.33 a month, although the loan is paid off a decade sooner and costs $9,331.87 less in interest.

The comparison uses year-one, flat-price savings and values every kWh at retail. If your utility credits exports for less, savings will be lower; if prices rise, they will grow. Model those effects in the solar payback calculator, and check the loan amount against the cost calculator for your state.

Loan, lease or cash: what changes?

Ownership changes who gets the savings and any incentives. With a loan or cash, you own the system and its output. With a lease or power purchase agreement (PPA), a solar company owns it and you pay for its use.

  • Cash: no interest, so payback is fastest, but it ties up the full cost.
  • Loan: spreads the cost; the U.S. Department of Energy notes loans can lower up-front costs (DOE). Interest adds to the total, as the table above shows.
  • Lease: fixed monthly payments based on estimated output, often with no money down, according to DOE.
  • PPA: you buy the system’s power at a set price per kWh.

The FTC notes that some federal, state or local incentives are not available if you lease or sign a PPA (FTC). Rules differ by program: New York’s state credit, for instance, can apply to certain leases and to PPAs of at least ten years (NY Tax Department). The FTC also advises getting detailed bids from several companies and never signing under pressure. For financing tailored to you, consider speaking with a lender or financial adviser.

Frequently asked questions

Is there still a 30% solar tax credit in 2026?

Not for new home systems. The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025 (IRS). If a loan offer is structured around a credit you expect to receive, ask how the payment changes when no credit is paid toward the balance.

How much is solar for a 2,000 sq ft house?

Floor area does not set the price; your electricity use does. At the 2022 U.S. average use of 10,791 kWh a year, a Texas system of about 7.1 kW costs roughly $16,047 before incentives. Financed over 20 years at 6.99%, a loan of that size would be about $124 a month.

Are solar loans a good idea?

A loan can make sense when monthly savings meet or beat the payment, as in the California example above. Where savings are smaller, as with a short term in Texas, you pay more each month than you save until the loan ends. Compare APRs, fees and terms from several lenders before deciding.
