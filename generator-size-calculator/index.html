Generator Size Calculator

This generator size calculator adds your running watts, the largest single starting surge and a safety margin. In the example, 1,800 running watts plus a 1,500 W surge makes a 3,300 W peak, so look for about 3,960 W at a 20% margin. Run portable units outdoors, at least 20 feet from the house (CPSC, 2026).

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Rooftop Payback

What size generator do I need to run my house?

Size the generator to the loads you will run at the same time, not to your whole house. List the essentials, add their running watts, add the largest extra starting surge, then apply a margin you choose.

The calculator’s example list works like this:

Refrigerator7002,2001,500
Sump pump8001,300500
Lights3003000
  1. Running load: 700 + 800 + 300 = 1,800 W.
  2. Peak: 1,800 + the largest surge, 1,500 W = 3,300 W.
  3. With a 20% margin: 3,300 × 1.2 = 3,960 W.

These values are placeholders, not measurements. Replace them with the figures on your appliance labels or manuals, since the same type of appliance can differ a lot between models.

Plan the list around outages you actually face. EIA reports U.S. customers averaged 11 hours without power in 2024, 80% of it from major events such as hurricanes (EIA). For longer outages, fuel supply matters as much as wattage.

What are starting watts and running watts?

Running watts are what an appliance draws while operating. Starting watts are the brief extra draw when a motor or compressor starts, which is why a refrigerator or pump can need far more power for a moment than it uses afterward.

The calculator adds only the single largest surge, on the assumption that motors start one at a time. In the example, the refrigerator’s 1,500 W extra surge is counted and the sump pump’s 500 W is not. If two large motors could start together, add the second surge yourself or increase the margin.

Resistive loads such as lights and heaters have no meaningful surge, so their starting and running watts are the same. Enter both columns for every item so the tool can find the largest difference.

For appliances rated in amps, multiply amps by volts for watts. To see what your loads cost or use in energy, list them in the electricity cost calculator, and convert energy with the kWh calculator.

Portable vs standby generator: how does sizing differ?

The sizing math is the same, but what you size for differs. A portable generator usually powers a short list through extension cords, so you size it for those items. A standby unit is wired into the home, so you size it for every circuit it will serve, and the calculator notes it needs a licensed installer and a transfer switch.

Safety rules shape the choice. CPSC says to run portable generators outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the house, and never inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace or shed; it reports nearly 100 U.S. deaths a year from carbon monoxide poisoning linked to portable generators (CPSC, February 19, 2026).

CPSC also warns never to power house wiring by plugging a generator into a wall outlet, known as backfeeding, which it calls “extremely dangerous” because it puts utility workers and neighbors at risk of electrocution (CPSC safety alert). Use outdoor-rated extension cords with a wattage rating above the connected load.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate what size generator I need?

Add the running watts of everything you will power at the same time, add the largest extra starting surge among them, and multiply by 1 plus your safety margin. For 1,800 running watts and a 1,500 W surge, that is 3,300 W, or 3,960 W with a 20% margin.

What will a 7,500 watt generator run in a house?

With a 20% margin, a 7,500 W generator suits loads whose running watts plus largest surge total up to 6,250 W (7,500 ÷ 1.2). That is almost twice the 3,300 W peak in the example list, leaving room for more appliances. Add your own labels’ figures to see how close you come.

Can a 5,000W generator run a house?

It can run a list of essentials, but rarely everything at once. With a 20% margin, 5,000 W covers a combined peak of about 4,167 W, enough for the example list’s refrigerator, sump pump and lights at 3,300 W, with some room left. Large loads such as central air or electric heat may need a bigger unit.
