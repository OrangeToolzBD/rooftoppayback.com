Solar Payback Calculator

Are solar panels worth it? For a 7 kW system with no incentives, simple payback runs from 3.9 years in Hawaii to 21.3 years in North Dakota, with a middle state (Illinois) at 10.7 years. Those figures use EIA June 2026 electricity prices, PVGIS sunlight data and EnergySage installed costs. Enter your own numbers to see yours.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Rooftop Payback

How long does it take for solar panels to pay for themselves?

Most home systems in the U.S. pay for themselves in roughly 4 to 21 years, depending on the state. Payback is the point where the electricity you no longer buy adds up to what the system cost. Our state-by-state run puts 20 of 51 jurisdictions (the states plus DC) under 10 years, with the median at 10.7 years.

EnergySage, which surveys installer quotes, puts typical breakeven at “about 10 years” nationally (EnergySage, updated June 30, 2026). That lines up with the middle of our table, but the spread around it is wide, which is why a national average tells you little about your own roof.

The calculator works year by year. Each year’s savings are the kWh the panels make, times your electricity price, times the share of that price your utility credits. It adds those savings up until the running total covers the net cost, then interpolates inside the year when that happens. If the total never gets there within 40 years, it says so. It also shows 25-year savings and the net gain after cost.

What makes payback faster or slower in your state?

Three numbers do most of the work: what you pay per kWh, how much sun your roof gets, and what the system costs per watt. A high electricity price shortens payback the most, which is why expensive-power states lead the list even when they are not the sunniest.

  • Electricity price. Residential prices in June 2026 ranged from 13.11¢ per kWh in Nevada to 52.72¢ in Hawaii, about four times higher (EIA Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A). Every kWh your panels make is worth that price.
  • Sunlight. Modelled yearly output per kW of panels ranges from 622 kWh at Juneau, Alaska, to 1,841 kWh at Santa Fe, New Mexico (PVGIS 5.2, state capitals, optimal tilt, 14% losses). Arizona ranks 10th for payback at a below-average 15.18¢ because its panels produce so much.
  • Installed cost. EnergySage state averages run from $2.12 per watt in Florida to $4.19 in North Dakota (EnergySage, September 2026). North Dakota’s high cost and low price give it the slowest payback in our table.

Shade, an east- or west-facing roof, your actual bill rate or a lower quote can all move your answer, so every default can be overwritten. See the full ranking on the solar panel cost by state page.

Worked example: a 7 kW system in Texas, line by line

With Texas defaults, a 7 kW system costs $15,750, saves about $1,688 in year one, and pays back in 9.3 years. Here is each step, using the numbers the calculator loads for Texas: $2.25 per watt (EnergySage, September 2026), 1,513 kWh per kW per year (PVGIS, modelled at Austin) and 15.94¢ per kWh (EIA, June 2026).

StepCalculationResult
System cost7 kW × 1,000 × $2.25$15,750
Incentivesnone entered$0
Year-one output7 kW × 1,513 kWh10,591 kWh
Year-one savings10,591 kWh × $0.1594 × 100%$1,688
Payback$15,750 ÷ $1,688 per year9.3 years
25-year savings$1,688.21 × 25, flat prices$42,205
Net gain after 25 years$42,205 − $15,750$26,455

These defaults hold prices flat and assume no panel wear. Research reviewed by NREL found a median panel degradation rate of 0.5% a year across nearly 2,000 measurements (Jordan and Kurtz, 2012). U.S. residential electricity averaged 18.34¢ in June 2026, up from 17.47¢ a year earlier, a 5.0% rise (EIA). One year is not a forecast, but it shows why the escalation input matters.

With 3% yearly price growth and 0.5% yearly degradation, the same Texas system pays back in 8.5 years and saves about $57,553 over 25 years. Degradation alone, at 0.5%, stretches payback to 9.5 years.

How does net metering change the answer?

Net metering decides what each solar kWh is worth, and a lower export credit stretches payback. The calculator assumes 100% by default, meaning every kWh is valued at your full retail price. If your utility pays less for power you send to the grid, lower the “value of each solar kWh” input.

Rules differ by state and utility. In California, customers applying for interconnection since April 15, 2023 are on a net billing tariff that credits exports at their value to the grid, which the state commission says “is usually lower than the retail rate” (CPUC). In Minnesota, systems under 40 kW are compensated at the average utility energy rate, and the commission notes such rates are usually below the average retail energy rate (Minnesota PUC).

The blended value depends on how much of your output you use at the moment it is produced, since that power offsets a purchase at full price. With Texas defaults, valuing solar at 75% of retail moves payback from 9.3 to 12.4 years. At 50%, it reaches 18.7 years. Read the net metering guide for how to find your utility’s rate.

Is solar still worth it without the federal tax credit?

Often yes, but it takes longer to break even. The IRS states that “the credit is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025” (IRS, Residential Clean Energy Credit, page reviewed July 4, 2026). So every figure on this page assumes no federal credit, and the incentive input starts at $0.

For scale: had the Texas system above still qualified for a 30% credit, its net cost would have been $11,025 and payback about 6.5 years. Without it, payback is 9.3 years. States with high electricity prices remain fast: California comes out at 4.4 years and New York at 7.4 years with no incentives at all.

State and utility programs can still cut the net cost. Enter any you qualify for in the incentives box. The solar incentives guide explains where to look them up, and the 2026 tax credit page covers what the federal change means if your system was installed in 2025.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate solar payback?

Divide the net system cost by the yearly savings. Net cost is the installed price minus any incentives. Yearly savings are the kWh the system produces times your electricity price, adjusted for how your utility credits exports. A $15,750 system saving $1,688 a year pays back in about 9.3 years. If you expect prices to rise or output to decline, count savings year by year instead, as this calculator does.

What is the solar 120% rule?

It is an electrical code limit on how much solar can connect to your main panel. NYSERDA gives the formula as busbar rating × 1.2 minus main breaker rating, which equals the largest solar breaker allowed (NYSERDA fact sheet, 2025). A 100-amp busbar with a 100-amp main breaker allows 20 amps of solar. Larger systems may need a panel upgrade or a different connection, which adds cost and lengthens payback.

What is a good payback time for solar?

There is no official benchmark, so compare it with your own plans. Our state table has a median of 10.7 years for a 7 kW system without incentives, and EnergySage cites about 10 years nationally (EnergySage, 2026). A payback well inside the time you expect to stay in the home leaves more years of savings. Also consider the panel warranty term on your quote.

Is the 30% solar tax credit going away in 2026?

It has already ended for new home systems. The IRS says the Residential Clean Energy Credit is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025 (IRS). A system finished in 2025 can still be claimed on a 2025 return. Its FAQ adds that installation completion, not the payment date, decides the year (IRS FS-2025-05).
