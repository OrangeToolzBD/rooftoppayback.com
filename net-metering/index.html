What Is Net Metering?

What is net metering? It is a billing arrangement in which your utility credits you for solar power your system sends to the grid. The U.S. Department of Energy describes it as an arrangement in which owners “are compensated for any solar power generation that is exported” (DOE). What each exported kWh earns varies by state and utility.

What is net metering?

Net metering lets a home solar system bank surplus power as a credit and use it later. When your panels make more than the house is using, the extra flows onto the grid through your meter. When they make less, at night for example, you draw power back. The utility nets the two against each other.

The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), a federal research lab, defines net energy metering as letting system owners export excess energy “receiving a credit in kilowatt-hours” (NLR, updated February 20, 2026). Credits can be applied within the current billing cycle or carried to future cycles.

Its purpose is to let a home use its own solar output across the day and the seasons without needing a battery to store it. For a system owner, it largely decides what solar power is worth, because a system can export much of its output when the home is using little.

Net metering is a state and utility policy, not a federal program. Each state commission or utility board sets its own terms, including system size limits, how long credits last, and what happens to leftover credits at year end.

How does net metering work on your bill?

On a net-metered bill, you pay for the kWh you drew from the grid minus the kWh you sent back, over the billing period. If the credit is worth the full retail rate, each exported kWh cancels one kWh you would otherwise buy.

An illustration with round numbers: suppose that in a month your home draws 900 kWh from the grid and your panels export 600 kWh. Under full-retail net metering you are billed for the 300 kWh difference. If you exported more than you drew, the surplus would become a credit toward later bills.

What happens to credits left at the end of the year depends on the program. In Minnesota, the state commission says excess credits at public utilities are paid at the avoided cost rate at year end, while at cooperative and municipal utilities they are canceled with no extra pay (Minnesota PUC). California net billing customers carry excess bill credits forward monthly until an annual true-up (CPUC).

Your tariff also lists any fixed monthly charge or minimum bill, which affects whether a bill can reach zero even when net usage does.

What is the difference between net metering and net billing?

Net metering nets your imports and exports over a billing period, usually at the retail rate. Net billing nets them in real time: power you use as it is produced offsets your purchases, and only the surplus is exported and credited, typically at a lower sell rate. NLR notes that net billing does not typically allow banking of kWh within a billing cycle (NLR).

The sell rate is the key difference. NLR lists options such as the retail rate, an avoided cost rate reflecting what the utility would pay to generate or buy the power, and a value-of-solar tariff (NLR). A third arrangement, buy-all sell-all, sends all solar output to the grid at a set sell rate while you buy everything you use at retail.

California shows the change in practice. Customers applying for interconnection since April 15, 2023 take service on a net billing tariff under decision D.22-12-056, with exports credited at a value the CPUC says is usually below retail but can rise above it on late summer evenings. The commission adds that nearly 70% of net billing customers had paired batteries with solar by the end of 2024 (CPUC).

For payback, net billing means the share of output you use yourself matters more. The solar payback calculator handles this with one input: the average value of each solar kWh as a percentage of your retail price.

How do I find my utility’s export rate?

Your export rate is set in your utility’s tariff, which is approved by your state utility commission or, for cooperatives and municipal utilities, by the utility’s own board. Start with DSIRE, which lists net metering and related policies by state, then open the utility’s tariff page for the current rate.

  1. Look on your bill for your rate schedule name and your price per kWh (total bill ÷ kWh used).
  2. Search your utility’s website for “net metering,” “net billing,” or “distributed generation” tariff.
  3. Find the credit for exported energy and how leftover credits are handled at year end.
  4. Check the state commission site for pending changes, since export rules are revised through public proceedings.

Then estimate your blended value. If about half your output is used at home at the full retail price and half is exported at 50% of retail, the average is 75%. With Texas defaults, a 7 kW system pays back in 9.3 years at 100% and 12.4 years at 75%. Estimate what your system will export with the solar panel calculator.

Frequently asked questions

What are the downsides of net metering?

The rules can change, and leftover credits may be worth less than you expect. California moved new customers from retail-rate net metering to net billing in April 2023. In Minnesota, cooperative and municipal utilities cancel year-end excess credits without payment. A payback estimate made under one tariff may not hold if your utility revises it.

Is net metering worth it for homeowners?

Where it credits exports at or near retail, it usually gives solar owners the most value for their output, since every surplus kWh offsets a purchase. Where exports earn less, the value falls and using more of your output at home, or adding storage, matters more. Enter your blended credit in the payback calculator to compare.

Do I need a battery if I have net metering?

Not to save money under full-retail net metering, because the grid effectively stores your surplus as a credit. A battery adds value where exports earn less than retail, or if you want stored power for outages. Size one with the battery calculator.
