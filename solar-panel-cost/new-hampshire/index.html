Solar Panel Cost in New Hampshire

In New Hampshire, a 7 kW home solar system costs about $20,160 before incentives at $2.88 per watt (EnergySage, September 2026). At 27.01¢ per kWh (EIA, June 2026), it pays for itself in about 8.1 years.

What solar incentives are available in New Hampshire?

New Hampshire no longer offers a state rebate for home solar. The Department of Energy says its residential renewable electrical generation rebate is permanently closed after being repealed in 2024 (NH Department of Energy), and all of its rebate programs are closed to new projects until further notice under the 2025 budget law (NH Department of Energy rebates page). Both were checked September 16, 2026.

The remaining help is local. Towns and cities may adopt a property tax exemption for solar and energy storage, and the amount varies by town (DSIRE, updated April 14, 2026). Dover, for example, offers a residential exemption based on the assessed value of the panels, with applications due each year by April 15 (City of Dover). Ask your own assessor whether your town has adopted it.

We did not find a statewide sales tax exemption to report, and New Hampshire's low-to-moderate income solar grants fund community projects through competitive requests rather than paying individual homeowners.

The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit no longer helps here: the IRS says it "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025" (IRS, checked September 16, 2026). With no federal credit and no state rebate, a New Hampshire system is paid back mainly through bill savings and export credits, covered below.

How does net metering work in New Hampshire?

New Hampshire offers net metering for home and small commercial systems up to 100 kW AC behind the meter, for Eversource, Liberty, the New Hampshire Electric Co-op and Unitil customers (NH Department of Energy, checked September 16, 2026).

Exports are not credited at the full retail price. Under the current tariff, often called Net Metering 2.0, systems up to 100 kW earn 100% of the supply rate, 100% of the transmission rate and 25% of the distribution rate for power sent to the grid (Community Power Coalition of NH presentation, April 2025; DSIRE, updated April 14, 2026). DSIRE also lists payout options for accumulated credit, yearly above $100 or quarterly above $25.

The Public Utilities Commission kept this tariff in an order issued November 18, 2024 (Community Power Coalition statement). A net metering bill, SB 106, did not become law; the House voted 172 to 152 on January 8, 2026 to postpone it indefinitely (FastDemocracy bill record).

Because 75% of the distribution charge is dropped on exports, power you use at home is worth more than power you send out. Our net metering guide explains the difference.

Is solar worth it in New Hampshire?

New Hampshire ranks 8 of 51 in our model. A 7 kW system at $2.88 per watt costs an estimated $20,160, produces about 9,233 kWh a year at Concord and saves about $2,494 in year one at 27.01¢ per kWh. The simple payback is 8.1 years with no incentives and flat prices.

That figure assumes every kWh is worth the full retail price. Under Net Metering 2.0, only the power you use yourself gets that; exports lose most of the distribution charge. How much that matters depends on how much you export and how large the distribution part of your bill is. As an illustration, if your solar averaged 75% of retail value, payback would be about 10.8 years.

There is no state rebate to subtract. A local property tax exemption, where your town offers one, keeps your tax bill from rising but is not in our figure.

These are estimates. Eversource, Liberty, Unitil and the Co-op have different prices, and your roof may differ from the Concord model. Look at your bill's supply, transmission and distribution lines, estimate your export value, and enter it in the solar payback calculator to see your own range.
