What does solar cost in each state?

Before incentives, a 7 kW system costs between $14,840 and $29,330 depending on the state, and most states fall between $16,000 and $21,000. The national average is about $2.60 per watt (EnergySage, June 30, 2026), or $18,200 for 7 kW.

The cheapest states per watt in the table are Florida ($2.12), Texas ($2.25), Arizona ($2.28) and Mississippi ($2.29). The most expensive are North Dakota ($4.19), Alabama ($3.84), Nebraska ($3.59) and Wyoming ($3.55). Alaska has no EnergySage state figure, so its row uses the national $2.60 and is marked with an asterisk.

Cost is only half the picture. Two states with the same price can have very different paybacks because electricity prices and sunlight differ. A system is worth more where each kWh it makes replaces expensive grid power. Texas and Colorado, for example, pay back in 9.3 and 9.4 years, though Colorado’s installed price is $2.65 per watt against $2.25 in Texas, because Colorado panels are modelled to produce more and its power costs more.

Your own quote may land above or below your state average. Use the solar panel cost calculator to size a system from your usage, then compare it with real bids.

Where does solar pay back fastest?

Hawaii and California pay back fastest by a wide margin, at 3.9 and 4.4 years for a 7 kW system with no incentives. Both have high electricity prices: 52.72¢ and 34.74¢ per kWh in June 2026 (EIA Table 5.6.A), against a U.S. average of 18.34¢.

Twenty of the 51 rows pay back in under 10 years, and the median is 10.7 years (Illinois). Washington ranks 46th at 15.3 years: its capital has the second-lowest modelled output in the table, and its power costs 14.91¢. Try your own numbers in the solar payback calculator.

How were these numbers calculated?

Every row uses the same assumptions so states can be compared fairly: a 7 kW system, no incentives, today’s electricity price held flat, and no panel degradation. Payback is the year the cumulative savings equal the system cost.

  • Price per watt: EnergySage state cost pages, September 2026 (EnergySage).
  • Electricity price: average residential price for June 2026 from EIA’s Electric Power Monthly, released August 26, 2026 (EIA).
  • Production: PVGIS 5.2 from the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre, 1 kW of crystalline panels at the state capital, optimal tilt, 14% losses.

For each state: cost = 7 × 1,000 × price per watt; yearly savings = 7 × kWh per kW × price. Texas, for example: $15,750 cost and $1,688 a year in savings, for 9.3 years.

These are estimates, not quotes. Real payback moves with your own rate, roof, shade, export credit and any incentives. Rising electricity prices would shorten every figure; lower export credits would lengthen them. The formulas and refresh schedule are on the methodology page.

Frequently asked questions

How much does solar cost for a 2,000 square foot house?

It depends on how much electricity the house uses rather than its size. At the 2022 U.S. average of 10,791 kWh a year, a system would be about 7.1 kW in Texas and 6.5 kW in California, roughly $16,000 to $16,500 before incentives in either state. Enter your own 12 months of kWh in the cost calculator for a closer figure.

Can a house run 100% on solar?

Over a year, panels can produce as much as a home uses if the system is sized to your yearly kWh and the roof has room. Hour by hour, though, you still draw from the grid at night and on cloudy days unless you add batteries. How your utility credits the surplus you export decides how much of your bill that offsets.

Why is my electric bill so high if I have solar?

Common reasons are exports credited below the retail rate, use that happens after dark, and a system sized below your current usage. In California, net billing credits exports at their grid value, which the CPUC says is usually below retail (CPUC). Compare your bill’s kWh with the system’s production report to see which applies.

Is solar really worth the money?

In Hawaii, California and Northeast states such as Rhode Island, Massachusetts and New York, a 7 kW system pays back in under eight years even with no incentives. Where power is cheap and installation is costly, it can take over 18 years.
