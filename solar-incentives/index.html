Solar Incentives by State (2026)

Solar incentives in 2026 come from states, utilities and local programs, not the federal residential credit, which the IRS says is unavailable for property placed in service after December 31, 2025 (IRS). Search DSIRE by state or ZIP code, then confirm each program on its own official page.

Which solar incentives are still available in 2026?

What remains in 2026 is a patchwork of state tax credits, sales and property tax exemptions, performance payments such as SRECs, and utility rebates. Which ones you can use depends entirely on where you live and who your utility is.

The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit covered 30% of costs for 2022 to 2025 (IRS), but not for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025. The IRS FAQ treats the spending as made when installation is completed (IRS FS-2025-05). See the 2026 tax credit page for the details.

How do state tax credits and rebates work?

A state tax credit reduces the state income tax you owe, while a rebate is cash paid toward the system cost. Both lower your net cost, but they work on different timelines: a rebate usually arrives near installation, a credit when you file your return.

Credits often have a percentage, a cap and rules about unused amounts. New York’s credit is 25% of qualified spending, limited to $5,000, is not refundable, and unused amounts can be carried over for up to five years (NY Tax Department). The system must be installed and used at your principal residence in the state. New York also allows the credit for certain leases and for power purchase agreements of at least ten years.

Tax exemptions work differently: they remove a tax instead of paying you. Washington’s exemption covers sales and use tax on qualifying solar equipment and, if the installer meets state requirements, on installation charges (WA DOR).

For a state-average 7 kW system in New York costing $19,320, the 25% credit works out to $4,830, under the cap. Entered in the solar payback calculator, it shortens payback from 7.4 years to about 5.5, if you have enough state tax to use it. For how a credit fits your own taxes, consider asking a tax professional.

What are SRECs?

SRECs, or solar renewable energy certificates, are credits that represent the clean-energy attribute of solar power, sold separately from the electricity. The EPA says SRECs “are created for each megawatt-hour of electricity generated from solar energy systems” (EPA, updated July 13, 2026).

Some states require electricity suppliers to meet part of their renewable portfolio standard with solar, a “solar carve-out.” Suppliers buy SRECs from solar owners to comply. The EPA notes that SREC value in these markets is set by supply and demand, driven largely by suppliers’ solar requirements or the compliance payment they would owe instead.

New Jersey’s current program pays a fixed value per certificate. Under its Administratively Determined Incentive program, net-metered residential registrations received on or after July 27, 2026 earn $77 per SREC-II, down from $85 (NJ Clean Energy Program). A state-average 7 kW New Jersey system is modelled to produce about 9.6 MWh a year (7 kW × 1,370 kWh), which would be worth roughly $738 a year at $77. Actual certificates depend on metered output.

How to check a program is still open

Check the program’s own page, not a summary, and look for its status, deadline and funding. Incentives close when budgets or capacity blocks fill, and values change by commission order, as New Jersey’s residential SREC-II value did in 2026.

  1. Find candidates on DSIRE by entering your state or ZIP code.
  2. Open the administering agency’s page: the state tax department, energy office, utility commission or your utility.
  3. Look for a status line. New Jersey’s ADI page, for example, says residential registrations are open until the block is fully subscribed or June 1, 2027, whichever comes first.
  4. Read the eligibility rules, especially timing. New Jersey prohibits commencing construction, defined as receiving permission to operate, before a project is accepted into its ADI program.
  5. Ask any installer to put the incentive amount, and who is responsible if it is not received, in writing.

Then compare the result with and without the incentive in the payback calculator so a missed program does not change whether the project makes sense.

Frequently asked questions

What solar incentives are available in New Jersey?

New Jersey’s main residential program is the Administratively Determined Incentive program, which pays through SREC-IIs tied to your system’s output. Residential registrations received on or after July 27, 2026 earn $77 per SREC-II, and registration is open until the block fills or June 1, 2027 (NJ Clean Energy Program). Check DSIRE for other state and utility programs.

Does Washington State have solar incentives?

Washington exempts qualifying solar equipment from sales and use tax for systems from 1 kW to 100 kW AC purchased through December 31, 2029, and installation charges can qualify if the installer meets state requirements. Utility programs vary, so search DSIRE and your utility’s site for anything else that applies.

Can I combine state incentives?

Sometimes, but each program sets its own eligibility rules, so check them one by one. A Washington sales tax exemption and a utility rebate, for example, come from different agencies with separate conditions. Read every program’s eligibility section before counting on the total, and consider asking a tax professional how any credits fit your return.
