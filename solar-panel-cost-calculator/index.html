Solar Panel Cost Calculator

This solar panel cost calculator multiplies system size by your state’s average installed price per watt. A 6 kW system in California at $2.52 per watt comes to about $15,120 before incentives (EnergySage, September 2026). Enter your yearly kWh instead and the tool sizes the system from your state’s modelled sunlight first.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Rooftop Payback

How much do solar panels cost for a home?

Nationally, installed home solar averages about $2.60 per watt before incentives, according to EnergySage’s June 30, 2026 update (EnergySage). At that rate, each kW of panels costs about $2,600, so a 7 kW system runs near $18,200 and a 10 kW system near $26,000 before any rebates.

EnergySage also publishes averages by system size, and larger systems cost less per watt:

The federal residential credit no longer applies to systems placed in service after December 31, 2025, so these are also roughly your out-of-pocket costs unless a state or utility program applies. What size system do I need?

Size the system from your electricity use, not your floor area. Add up 12 months of kWh from your bills and divide by how much one kW of panels produces a year where you live. The calculator does this division for you when you fill in the usage box.

The average U.S. home bought 10,791 kWh in 2022 (EIA). Sized to that figure, a Texas system comes to 7.1 kW (10,791 ÷ 1,513 kWh per kW) and about $16,047 at $2.25 per watt. The same usage in California needs 6.5 kW (10,791 ÷ 1,650) and costs about $16,481 at $2.52 per watt. Sunnier states need fewer panels for the same bill.

Production figures come from the European Commission’s PVGIS model at each state capital, with optimal tilt and 14% system losses. A shaded roof or panels facing east or west will produce less, so a real design may need to be larger. To turn kW into a panel count, use the solar panel calculator.

Why does the price per watt differ by state?

State averages range from $2.12 per watt in Florida to $4.19 in North Dakota, based on EnergySage’s state pages for September 2026 (EnergySage). On a 7 kW system, that gap is $14,840 versus $29,330.

Prices reflect local labor rates, permitting and interconnection work, and how many installers compete in a market. EnergySage publishes no state figure for Alaska, so the tool uses the national $2.60 average there and says so in the result.

A low price per watt does not always mean a fast payback. North Dakota pairs its high cost with 14.12¢ electricity (EIA, June 2026), for the slowest payback in our table. Hawaii costs $3.43 per watt, above average, yet pays back fastest because power there averaged 52.72¢ (EIA Table 5.6.A). Compare both sides on the cost by state table.

What is included in an installed price?

An installed price covers more than the panels. EnergySage’s breakdown includes panels, inverters, racking and wiring, plus labor, permitting, interconnection fees, sales and marketing, and installer overhead (EnergySage, 2026). By its estimate, the panels themselves are only about 12% of the total price.

That is why panel prices alone say little about what you will pay. Two quotes with the same panels can differ widely on labor, electrical work and margin.

Items that often sit outside the quoted price, or appear as extras, include a main panel upgrade, roof repairs before installation, and battery storage. A battery changes the cost a lot; size one with the battery calculator. The FTC suggests you “compare detailed bids from several companies” before signing (FTC, April 2022). Read each quote for what is included, the price per watt, and the expected yearly kWh.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a solar system for a 2,000 sq ft house?

Square footage does not set the size; your electricity use does. A home using the 2022 U.S. average of 10,791 kWh a year (EIA) would need about 7.1 kW in Texas, roughly $16,047 before incentives, or 6.5 kW in California, about $16,481. Enter your own 12-month usage in the calculator for a state-specific estimate.

Will solar prices go down in 2026?

No one can promise that. EnergySage said in its June 2026 update that solar prices “are about the lowest they’ve been,” and put them about 22% below a decade earlier, after a rise in 2020 to 2023 tied to supply-chain problems (EnergySage). Waiting also means paying your full electricity bill in the meantime.

Is solar worth it financially?

It depends mostly on your electricity price and sunlight. In our state table, a 7 kW system without incentives pays back in 3.9 years in Hawaii, 4.4 in California and 9.3 in Texas, but over 18 years in Nebraska, Tennessee and North Dakota. Run the payback calculator with your own bill rate and quote to see where you fall.
